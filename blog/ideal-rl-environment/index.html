What Is the Ideal RL Environment?

Verifiable rewards are not the same as binary rewards. The more useful question is whether the quality of a rollout, state, action, or trajectory can be evaluated externally according to an objective criterion.

HumanDotPy September 2, 2026

What does the ideal reinforcement learning environment look like? I would start with three properties:

  1. Verifiable rewards.
  2. Reducibility.
  3. Tractable credit assignment.

These properties are related, but they are not interchangeable. An environment may have a perfectly objective terminal reward and still provide an almost useless learning signal across a long trajectory. The first distinction to make is therefore between a reward being verifiable and a reward being binary.

1. Verifiable rewards

Verifiable rewards. Wow, so surprising. Reinforcement Learning with Verifiable Rewards has been the defining trend in reasoning-model post-training since GRPO was introduced in DeepSeekMath, and especially since the release of OpenAI's o1 series. But the underlying pattern is older than the current name. Chess, shogi, Atari, Dota, and similar environments all ground learning in outcomes that the environment can check.

I use RLVR in the broader sense: it applies whenever the quality of a rollout, state, action, or trajectory can be evaluated externally by a non-learned verifier according to an objective criterion. Put simply, RLVR is reinforcement learning where the reward is grounded in an objectively checkable evaluation.

V(τ, G) → ℝ

Here, τ = (s0, a0, s1, …, sT) is a trajectory and G is the task specification. This definition covers AIME, chess, kernels, Factorio, and even Dota. The verifier does not need to return only zero or one. It may check whether an answer is correct, whether a game was won, whether a factory sustained a target production rate, or whether one valid program is mechanically better than another.

That last case can be written as an improvement objective:

J(Si; G) > J(Si−1; G)

where J is mechanically measurable. The reward is verifiable even when the optimization target is continuous and even when progress is relative to a previous state. “Verifiable” describes the grounding of the evaluation, not the shape of the reward.

Why kernels are the premium example

Over the course of this essay I will explain why I believe GPU kernel optimization is the premium example of what an RL environment should look like. For a simple latency objective, correctness acts as a hard feasibility constraint and performance supplies the dense ordering:

J(Ki; G) = { −latency(Ki; G) if Ki is functionally correct −∞ otherwise

Latency is only shorthand. The real objective may combine time to first token, time between tokens, tokens per second, end-to-end wall-clock time, memory use, and workload-specific service-level constraints. What matters is that the objective and its constraints can be measured by the system rather than inferred by a learned judge.

Evaluation-complete states

Kernel optimization also gives us a useful distinction from chess and shogi. A chess or shogi position is a complete Markov state: it contains the information needed to determine the distribution of the next state. But it is not generally evaluation-complete under the actual objective of winning the game.

Define a state S as evaluation-complete for objective O when the true task objective can be measured from S without requiring the policy to continue the task. Evaluation may still require computation. It does not require another policy rollout.

Ki→run benchmark→O(Ki)

Each executable, correct candidate kernel is generally evaluation-complete in this sense. If the measured latency of a candidate is 37.2 microseconds, we can modify it and immediately evaluate the next candidate:

Ki ai→ Ki+1
ΔJi = J(Ki+1; G) − J(Ki; G)
improvement ⇔ ΔJi > 0
The missing piece in the original notation: if O is raw latency, lower is better and an improvement produces a negative delta. Defining J = −latency keeps the convention that a positive delta means improvement. In practice, J is an estimate from repeated benchmark runs, because hardware measurements contain noise.

This is unusually clean. An action produces a new artifact; correctness can be checked; performance can be measured; and the marginal effect of the action is available immediately. Chess, Atari, Dota, and Factorio also have objective outcomes, but an intermediate state usually must be rolled forward before the true objective is known.

Immediate verifiability does not solve reinforcement learning by itself. A terminal Factorio factory can be judged objectively, yet a failure after a thirty-minute rollout says very little about which decision caused it. This is where the second property becomes important: the environment must be reducible into meaningful, independently evaluable improvements without destroying the long-horizon task we actually care about.
